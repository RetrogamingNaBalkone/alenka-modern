THE CLEAR DESK. Make room for one important paper,
and three less important papers will take its place.
THE QUIET CLOCK. A clock seems loudest when the house
is quiet and you have finally decided to get some sleep.
THE SHORTCUT. The quickest path across the garden
usually leads straight through the deepest puddle.
THE WEATHER. Leave your umbrella at home, and the sky
will find a reason to remind you where you left it.
THE EMPTY CUP. A good idea often arrives just after
the tea is finished and the kettle has gone cold.
THE TIDY SHELF. Books stay in perfect order until
somebody needs the one that holds the others upright.
THE OPEN WINDOW. Fresh air is welcome; loose papers
tend to welcome it with rather too much enthusiasm.
THE SIMPLE PLAN. A task looks easy when someone else
has already done the difficult part.
THE PATIENT GARDENER. Pulling on a blade of grass
does not persuade the lawn to grow any faster.
THE HELPFUL LABEL. A box marked 'Useful Things'
will contain everything except the thing you need.
THE EARLY START. Getting ready takes five minutes;
finding your other glove takes the rest of the morning.
THE GOOD CHAIR. The most comfortable chair belongs
to the cat whenever you are ready to sit down.
THE LONG STORY. Ask for a short explanation,
and someone will begin with last year's summer holiday.
THE SMALL REPAIR. Fix one loose board, and the next
board will politely ask for the same attention.
THE CAREFUL TYPIST. Slow, steady practice saves time
that hurried fingers would spend correcting mistakes.
THE FRIENDLY HOUSE. A lamp, a book, a cup of tea:
small things can make a long day end well.
THE LAST WORD. Finish the line, relax your hands,
and begin the next one with a little more confidence.

The weary Mole also was glad to turn in without delay, and soon had
his head on his pillow, in great joy and contentment. But ere he
closed his eyes he let them wander round his old room, mellow in the
glow of the firelight that played or rested on familiar and friendly
things which had long been unconsciously a part of him, and now
smilingly received him back, without rancour. He was now in just the
frame of mind that the tactful Rat had quietly worked to bring about
in him.

He saw clearly how plain and simple--how narrow, even--it all was; but
clearly, too, how much it all meant to him, and the special value of
some such anchorage in one's existence. He did not at all want to
abandon the new life and its splendid spaces, to turn his back on sun
and air and all they offered him and creep home and stay there; the
upper world was all too strong, it called to him still, even down
there, and he knew he must return to the larger stage.

But it was good to think he had this to come back to; this place which
was all his own, these things which were so glad to see him again and
could always be counted upon for the same simple welcome.
